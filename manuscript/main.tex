\title{Streaming LLM Weights from CXL Memory: Regimes, Placement and Pipeline-Aware Pinning}

\author[buv]{Quang-Vinh Dang\corref{cor1}}
\ead{vinh.dq4@buv.edu.vn}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author. ORCID: 0000-0002-3877-8024}
\address[buv]{British University Vietnam, Hung Yen, Vietnam}

\begin{abstract}
Models whose weights exceed GPU memory can be served by streaming part of the weights from CXL-attached memory. A CXL Type-3 expander is not addressable by the GPU, so every transfer is a host-initiated DMA limited by the slower of the CXL device and the PCIe link (10--20~GB/s on published prototypes). We study this path with an event-driven simulator whose link and overlap logic is checked on a real GPU (pinned host memory stands in for the pool; no CXL device was measured). First, streaming is nearly free only above about 16k tokens per iteration: at 18~GB/s a 70B model is \nRegSlowSixteenKEighteen$\times$ slower than resident weights at 16k tokens and \nRegSlowOneEighteen$\times$ slower at one token. Second, where resident layers sit matters more than which ones: contiguous resident sets idle the link when compute and transfer are comparable, while interleaved placements, such as a FlexGen-style per-layer split, stay within about 7\% of an implementation-independent lower bound. Third, we present \camp, a runtime policy combining a work-conserving prefetcher with stall-cost-aware pinning (SCP), a plan-and-verify choice of the resident set. On Llama-2/3 (up to 405B) and Gemma-2 configurations \camp{} is \nDemOverCampMin--\nDemOverCampMax$\times$ faster than demand paging and within \nDetLbMed--\nDetLbMax$\times$ of the lower bound without noise. Against the strongest baseline, a tuned per-layer split, it is ahead by up to \nExtOverCampMaxPct\% and behind by at most \nWorstLossExt\%: it ties on uniform dense models and wins where reuse or unit sizes are non-uniform (up to \nHetEncFg$\times$ on encoder--decoder inference). The compute-time estimate changes latency by under 1\% once calibrated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The weights of current large language models no longer fit in the memory of a single accelerator: Llama-3-70B needs 141~GB in FP16 and Llama-3.1-405B needs 812~GB, against 80~GB of HBM on a high-end GPU. The usual remedies are to shard the model over several GPUs, which multiplies the hardware bill, to quantise it, which changes the model, or to offload part of it to host DRAM, SSD or a remote tier and stream it back on demand \cite{sheng2023flexgen,rajbhandari2021infinity,alizadeh2024flash}. Compute Express Link (CXL) memory expanders add a new option: byte-addressable DDR capacity attached to the host over PCIe-class links, at lower cost per gigabyte than HBM and without the capacity ceiling of the DIMM slots \cite{pond2023,tpp2023}. The catch is bandwidth. Prototype Type-3 devices sustain on the order of 10--20~GB/s towards a GPU \cite{yoon2025tract,jang2026itme}, two orders of magnitude below HBM.

Two facts shape the problem and are easy to overlook when CXL memory is treated as a directly attached GPU memory tier. First, a CXL Type-3 expander is a CXL.mem endpoint behind the host root complex; a GPU cannot address it directly. Weights reach HBM through a host-initiated \texttt{cudaMemcpyAsync} from page-locked, CXL-backed host memory, so the achievable rate is set by the slower of the CXL device and the PCIe link, and every copy carries software and synchronisation costs \cite{yoon2025tract}. Second, the access order of transformer inference is static, but the compute available to hide a transfer is not: it scales with the number of tokens processed per iteration. Streaming a layer of $p$ parameters costs $2p$ bytes of link traffic and enables $2pT$ FLOPs for $T$ tokens, so a link of 18~GB/s hides the transfer only when $T$ is in the tens of thousands (Section~\ref{sec:model}). Prefill of long prompts and very large batches are in that regime; interactive decode is not.

Known-graph tensor swapping is an established idea in training \cite{rhu2016vdnn,huang2020swapadvisor,hildebrand2020autotm,peng2020capuchin,ren2021sentinel}, and layer-wise weight streaming with overlapped I/O is established in inference \cite{sheng2023flexgen,jang2026itme}. We do not claim these ideas as new. This paper asks what is left to decide, and how much it is worth, when LLM weights are streamed from CXL through the host path. We make three kinds of contributions, and are explicit about which are positive and which are negative results.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A path model, a threshold and a lower bound (Sections~\ref{sec:model}, \ref{sec:res-regime}).} A host-mediated transfer model with explicit initiator, bandwidth bound, per-copy and synchronisation costs and a staged (bounce-buffer) variant; a closed-form arithmetic-intensity threshold; an implementation-independent lower bound for whole-unit streaming; and a regime map showing that managed streaming comes within \nRegSlowSixteenKEighteen$\times$ of resident weights only above about 16k tokens per iteration (18~GB/s), while one-token decode is \nRegSlowOneEighteen$\times$ slower. This part needs no policy and is the part most likely to transfer to real hardware.
\item \textbf{Where the policy gains come from (Sections~\ref{sec:res-pins}, \ref{sec:res-attrib}).} Against a conventional hot/cold baseline, nearly all of the benefit is explained by \emph{placement}: contiguous resident sets idle the link when compute and transfer are comparable, and interleaved sets do not. A per-layer fractional split in the style of FlexGen is already interleaved and, in our simulator, stays within \nWorstLossExt\% of \camp{} on uniform dense models once its buffer count is tuned. A baseline that adds one ingredient at a time (Section~\ref{sec:res-attrib}) shows which ingredients matter.
\item \textbf{The \camp{} policy and the cases where it differs (Sections~\ref{sec:camp}, \ref{sec:res-main}, \ref{sec:res-reuse}).} \camp{} combines a work-conserving prefetcher with next-use admission, a pipeline planner and stall-cost-aware pinning (SCP), which scores candidate resident sets with the planner and verifies them in a dry run. SCP never lost more than \nScpRegretMaxPct\% to the best of six simpler pin rules in \nPinCfg{} configurations and gained up to \nScpGainOverBestSimpleMax\% over it, without being told which rule suits the workload. Against a FlexGen-style split whose buffer count and lookahead are tuned per configuration by simulation, \camp{} is within $\pm$\nWorstLossExt\% on \nTieExtPlusWins{} of \nMainRows{} rows and \nFgLoseMaxPct\% faster on Gemma-2 (tied embedding); where per-layer reuse differs strongly (encoder--decoder) the split is \nHetEncFg$\times$ slower. Against the conventional hot/cold baseline the gain reaches \nHcOverCampMax$\times$.
\item \textbf{Methodology and negative results (Sections~\ref{sec:method}, \ref{sec:validation}, \ref{sec:res-cost}).} An event-driven simulator with common random numbers, ten seeds, a hardware check of its link and overlap logic on a real GPU, and a cost-model study. The compute estimate, a prominent component in related proposals, affects latency by at most \nCmSpreadB\% once calibrated by one probe iteration, and an LFU eviction policy is indistinguishable from LRU on cyclic scans.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope} We target dense transformer models whose layer order is static. We do not evaluate Mixture-of-Experts routing, multi-GPU or multi-tenant sharing of a CXL pool, KV-cache placement in CXL, or CXL switches. No CXL hardware was available: the CXL link is simulated with parameters anchored in published measurements, and the hardware validation (Section~\ref{sec:validation}) uses pinned host memory on a PCIe-attached GPU as a stand-in for the pool. All GPU compute times other than the T4 micro-benchmark come from a roofline model, not from measurement.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reaching CXL memory from a GPU}
CXL expanders have been studied mainly as a CPU-side capacity tier: Pond \cite{pond2023} and TPP \cite{tpp2023} place or migrate pages between local DRAM and CXL memory, and DirectCXL \cite{directcxl2022} and the characterisations of Melody \cite{melody2025} and Sun et al.\ \cite{demystifying2023} report latencies of roughly 140--410~ns and device-dependent bandwidth. Work on GPUs is more recent and confirms the host-mediated nature of the path. TraCT \cite{yoon2025tract} reports that a plain \texttt{cudaMemcpy} from a CXL-mapped region goes through a bounce buffer in host DRAM and must be avoided by page-locking the region so that the DMA engine reads the device directly; it measures 10.1~GB/s and 640~ns for a second-generation expander. ITME \cite{jang2026itme} prefetches model weights layer by layer from a CXL-hybrid memory into GPU memory with a DMA pipeline and reports about 18~GB/s for the prototype. DAK \cite{lin2026dak} instead lets the GPU read remote memory directly through the Tensor Memory Accelerator on NVLink-C2C and PCIe systems, and cache-coherent GPU--CPU links such as GH200 expose coherent memory to the GPU \cite{wang2024hitchhiker}; these are different access paths from the one we model. Compression raises the effective bandwidth of a CXL link \cite{xie2025trace} and is orthogonal to the policy studied here. Full-system CXL simulators with silicon validation, such as CXL-DMSim \cite{wang2025cxldmsim}, model the device and host side in far more detail than we do but, to our knowledge, do not model GPU kernels.

\paragraph{Weight offloading for LLM inference}
FlexGen \cite{sheng2023flexgen} formulates the placement of weights, activations and KV cache over GPU, CPU and disk as a linear program with per-tensor percentages and overlaps I/O with compute for throughput-oriented generation; ZeRO-Inference/Infinity \cite{rajbhandari2021infinity} stream partitioned weights from CPU and NVMe; llama.cpp \cite{gerganov2023llamacpp} and vLLM \cite{kwon2023vllm} expose CPU offload of a fraction of the layers. LLM in a flash \cite{alizadeh2024flash} streams weights from flash with windowing and row--column bundling to reduce bytes moved. PowerInfer \cite{song2024powerinfer} keeps frequently activated (``hot'') neurons on the GPU and computes the rest on the CPU, exploiting activation sparsity rather than streaming weights; Fiddler \cite{kamahori2025fiddler} and MoE-Infinity \cite{xue2024moeinfinity} orchestrate CPU and GPU or cache experts by activation patterns for Mixture-of-Experts models. Serving systems batch iteration by iteration \cite{yu2022orca,kwon2023vllm}, and chunked prefill \cite{agrawal2024sarathi} mixes prompt chunks with decode tokens, which is what makes the arithmetic intensity of an iteration a tunable quantity. KV-cache offloading \cite{lee2024infinigen,qin2025mooncake} and prefill/decode disaggregation \cite{zhong2024distserve} address different data and are complementary.

\paragraph{Known-graph memory management}
When the future access order is known, Belady's optimal replacement \cite{belady1966study} gives the offline reference. Training systems exploit the dataflow graph to swap activations and tensors between GPU and host or persistent memory: vDNN \cite{rhu2016vdnn}, SwapAdvisor \cite{huang2020swapadvisor} (joint scheduling, allocation and swap decisions by genetic search), AutoTM \cite{hildebrand2020autotm} (integer linear programming for placement and movement), Capuchin \cite{peng2020capuchin} (runtime tensor-access profiling) and Sentinel \cite{ren2021sentinel} (migration on heterogeneous memory); MAGIS and MEMO \cite{chen2024magis,zhao2025memo} plan activation memory. Our setting differs in what is moved (read-only weights, no write-back), in the bandwidth regime (an order of magnitude below PCIe-attached DRAM for the same GPU) and in the access pattern (a cyclic scan with a regime set by the batch size), but the planning idea is the same, and we do not present known-graph prefetching itself as new. General tiering controllers infer behaviour from pressure or access samples \cite{tmo,limoncello}. Request-level LLM simulators such as Vidur \cite{agrawal2024vidur} model scheduling rather than the memory path.

\paragraph{Positioning}
Table~\ref{tab:positioning} summarises the closest systems. \camp{} differs from FlexGen in operating on whole units with an explicit transfer model and in choosing the resident set by a pipeline-aware objective and a verification step; our experiments show that the two are on par for uniform dense models and differ for non-uniform ones (Sections~\ref{sec:res-main} and \ref{sec:res-reuse}). We do not claim that frequency-based hot/cold partitioning is inferior in general.

\section{System model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Host-mediated CXL-to-GPU path}
\label{sec:path}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the path that the simulator models. The CXL Type-3 expander is exposed to the operating system as a CPU-less NUMA node. The \camp{} runtime, a host thread, page-locks the CXL-backed region holding the weights and issues one \texttt{cudaMemcpyAsync} per layer unit on a dedicated copy stream; the GPU copy engine then reads the data through the root complex, which forwards the reads to the expander using CXL.mem, and writes it into the HBM slot of the target layer. Kernels of the consuming layer wait on a CUDA event recorded after the copy. The initiator is therefore the host, the data crosses two links in series (CXL, then PCIe), and the sustained rate is bounded by the slower of the two:
\begin{equation}
\bwlink = \eta\,\min(BW_{\mathrm{CXL}},\,BW_{\mathrm{PCIe}}),
\qquad
t_{\mathrm{copy}}(s) = t_0 + \Big\lceil \tfrac{s}{c}\Big\rceil t_c + t_{\mathrm{sync}} + \frac{s}{\bwlink},
\label{eq:copy}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the transfer size, $c$ the DMA chunk size, $t_0$ the per-copy API, driver and first-byte cost, $t_c$ the per-chunk descriptor cost and $t_{\mathrm{sync}}$ the event synchronisation cost. If the region is \emph{not} page-locked, the driver stages data through a bounce buffer in host DRAM; the CPU copy and the DMA are then serial and $\bwlink = (1/BW + 1/BW_{\mathrm{stage}})^{-1}$ (``staged'' mode). On a Tesla T4 we measured 12.4~GB/s for pinned and 4.6~GB/s for pageable copies, a ratio of 0.37 (Section~\ref{sec:validation}), consistent with the bounce-buffer penalty reported for CXL regions \cite{yoon2025tract}.

The simulator additionally includes (i) a single non-preemptive copy engine that serves demand fetches before queued prefetches, (ii) log-normal noise on every copy and a slow autoregressive drift of the link bandwidth that models interference from other traffic, and (iii) a fixed host sampling gap between decode iterations during which the link keeps working. It does \emph{not} model CXL switches or pooling across hosts, interleaving of several expanders (this is folded into $\bwlink$, which we sweep from 8 to 64~GB/s), GPU-initiated direct access to remote memory, cache-coherence traffic, or contention with host CPU memory traffic. Table~\ref{tab:platform} lists the parameters and their basis.

\subsection{Layer units and iteration traces}
\label{sec:units}

A model is decomposed into \emph{units}, the granularity at which weights are fetched and pinned: one attention block (QKV and output projections and norms), one feed-forward block, the embedding table and the output head (tied models share one unit). Table~\ref{tab:models} lists the configurations. An \emph{iteration} is one pass over the units: a prefill pass over $B$ prompts of $S$ tokens, a decode step for $B$ sequences with context length $C$, or a mixed iteration in which a prefill chunk piggybacks on decode tokens. The embedding access is a gather of $T$ rows rather than a full-table fetch. The KV cache and workspace stay in HBM and reduce the capacity $C_w$ available for weights: for Llama-3-70B with $B{=}16$ sequences of up to 4k tokens, the KV cache takes 21.5~GB, leaving $C_w=54.5$~GB (39\% of the weights).

\subsection{Compute model and regimes}
\label{sec:compute}

The execution time of a unit on the simulated GPU follows a roofline model \cite{williams2009roofline} per sub-operation:
\begin{equation}
t = \max\!\Big(\frac{F_g}{P\,\eta_g},\,\frac{B_g}{W\,\eta_m}\Big) + \max\!\Big(\frac{F_a}{P\,\eta_a},\,\frac{B_a}{W\,\eta_m}\Big) + n_k\,t_{\mathrm{launch}},
\label{eq:roofline}
\end{equation}
where $F_g,B_g$ are the FLOPs and HBM bytes of the projections, $F_a,B_a$ those of the attention core (including the KV cache read), $P$ the peak FLOP rate, $W$ the HBM bandwidth, $\eta$ the efficiencies and $n_k$ the number of kernels. This is the \emph{ground truth} of the simulated device; \camp{} sees only the features $(F_g,B_g,F_a,B_a,n_k)$ and must estimate $t$ (Section~\ref{sec:costmodel}).

Streaming a unit of $p$ parameters moves $b\,p$ bytes ($b=2$ for FP16) and enables $2pT$ FLOPs, so compute hides the copy when
\begin{equation}
T \;\ge\; T^\star = (1-\varphi)\,\frac{P\,\eta_g}{\bwlink}\cdot\frac{b}{2},
\label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
with $\varphi$ the fraction of weight bytes kept resident. For $P\eta_g = 613$~TFLOP/s, $\varphi=0.4$ and $\bwlink=18$ or 45~GB/s, $T^\star\approx 20\mathrm{k}$ or $8\mathrm{k}$ tokens per iteration. Decode with $B\le 512$ is therefore always link-bound, and the only lever is the number of bytes that cross the link per iteration. Section~\ref{sec:res-regime} confirms this threshold in the simulator.

\subsection{A lower bound}
\label{sec:method-lb}

An iteration cannot finish before its compute-only time $\sum_u t_u$, nor before the minimum number of streamed bytes has crossed the link. If any unit is streamed, one slot as large as the largest streamed unit must stay free, so with $W_{\mathrm{tot}}$ total weight bytes and cache capacity $C_w$ at least $W_{\mathrm{tot}}-(C_w-s)$ bytes are streamed, where $s$ is the size of the largest streamed unit (all larger units must then be pinned and fit in $C_w-s$). We take the smallest feasible $s$:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{LB}} = \max\Big(\sum_u t_u,\ \frac{\max\{0,\,W_{\mathrm{tot}}-C_w+s_{\min}\}}{\bwlink}\Big).
\label{eq:lb}
\end{equation}
The bound ignores per-copy overheads and pipeline fill, and is exact in the link-bound regime for uniform units. It is implementation-independent for policies that move and pin \emph{whole} units: none can beat it, and it gives each result an absolute reference. A policy that splits every layer fractionally, as FlexGen's per-layer percentages do, can undercut it slightly (by up to \nFgBelowLbMax\% in our experiments), because the streaming slot then holds a fraction of a unit.

\section{The CAMP policy}
\label{sec:camp}

\camp{} has three parts. All rely on the static unit order of the model (the ``graph''), none on offline profiling of the model or workload.

\subsection{Runtime cost model}
\label{sec:costmodel}

The prefetcher and the pinning stage need an estimate $\hat t_u$ of each unit's compute time for the \emph{current} batch descriptor $(B,S,C)$. \camp{} computes it from tensor shapes with the roofline of Eq.~(\ref{eq:roofline}), using generic constants for the device class (efficiencies $\hat\eta_g=0.50$, $\hat\eta_m=0.80$, $\hat\eta_a=0.35$) and the peak rates that the driver reports. These constants differ from the device's true efficiencies, so the estimate is calibrated online: for each unit kind $\kappa$ (attention, feed-forward, embedding, head) the runtime keeps a multiplicative correction updated by an exponential moving average of the ratio between observed and nominal duration,
\begin{equation}
\hat c_\kappa \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,\hat c_\kappa + \alpha\,\frac{t^{\mathrm{obs}}_u}{t^{\mathrm{nom}}_u},\qquad \hat t_u = \hat c_\kappa\, t^{\mathrm{nom}}_u,\quad \alpha=0.3 .
\label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}
The first iteration therefore runs with the generic estimate, and every later iteration, or any re-planning after the first probe iteration, uses the calibrated one. The current layer's own duration is \emph{not} needed before it completes: the estimate of unit $u$ is available from its features before it runs, and the observation after it runs only refines future estimates. We quantify the effect of estimator error in Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}.

\subsection{Work-conserving prefetching with next-use admission}
\label{sec:prefetch}

Because the unit order is static, the next use of every unit is known. Algorithm~\ref{alg:prefetch} issues prefetches whenever the link or a layer completes. A fetch of the unit needed at position $j$ is admitted if free capacity exists or if some resident, unpinned unit has its next use \emph{after} $j$ (Belady-style admission \cite{belady1966study}); otherwise the scan stops. The demand fetch of the layer about to run is always issued first, and prefetches cannot take capacity while a demand fetch is waiting for room. This controller is work-conserving: it never leaves the link idle if capacity allows another useful fetch. An earlier formulation limited the lookahead to the units whose cumulative transfer time fits in the estimated remaining compute of the current layer; in all our workloads that gate never binds, because completions re-trigger the controller, and results are identical with and without it (Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}). The estimate $\hat t_u$ is thus not on the critical path of prefetching; it matters for pinning.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Work-conserving prefetch controller (invoked at layer start, layer begin and every copy completion)}
\label{alg:prefetch}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} access order $Q$, current position $i$, cache with pinned set $\mathcal P$
\State $j \gets i+1$
\While{$j < |Q|$ \textbf{and} no demand fetch is blocked}
  \State $u \gets Q[j]$
  \If{$u$ is neither resident nor in flight}
    \If{free capacity $\ge$ size$(u)$}
      \State issue prefetch$(u)$
    \Else
      \State $v \gets \arg\max_{w\ \mathrm{resident},\,w\notin\mathcal P,\,w\ne Q[i]}\ \mathrm{nextuse}(w)$
      \If{nextuse$(v) > j$} evict $v$; issue prefetch$(u)$ \textbf{else} \textbf{break} \EndIf
    \EndIf
  \EndIf
  \State $j \gets j+1$
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Stall-cost-aware pinning}
\label{sec:scp}

\paragraph{Problem statement}
Let $U$ be the set of units with sizes $s_u$, $C_w$ the weight-cache capacity, $\mathcal P\subseteq U$ the pinned set and $T_{\mathrm{plan}}(\mathcal P)$ the predicted steady-state iteration time of the pipeline planner defined below. SCP solves
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathcal P\subseteq U}\ T_{\mathrm{plan}}(\mathcal P)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{u\in\mathcal P}s_u + \max_{v\in U\setminus\mathcal P}s_v \;\le\; C_w ,
\label{eq:scp}
\end{equation}
that is, the pinned units plus one streaming slot large enough for the largest streamed unit must fit. This is \emph{not} a 0--1 knapsack: $T_{\mathrm{plan}}$ is not additive over units and depends on where the pinned units sit in the sequence. The additive proxy
\begin{equation}
\max_{\mathcal P}\ \sum_{u\in\mathcal P}F(u)\,t_{\mathrm{copy}}(s_u)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{u\in\mathcal P}s_u \le C_w - s^\ast ,
\label{eq:knap}
\end{equation}
with $F(u)$ the number of accesses of $u$ per iteration, is a 0--1 knapsack whose benefit-per-byte is $F(u)\,t_{\mathrm{copy}}(s_u)/s_u\approx F(u)/\bwlink$. For uniform sizes it ranks units by $F(u)$ alone, which is the frequency rule of \camp-v1 and of hot/cold partitioning. We solve Eq.~(\ref{eq:knap}) exactly by dynamic programming and use it as a baseline and as one SCP candidate; it is a proxy, and Section~\ref{sec:res-pins} shows when it is a poor one.

\paragraph{Pipeline planner}
$T_{\mathrm{plan}}(\mathcal P)$ is evaluated with a deterministic recurrence over one iteration repeated three times. Let $\hat c_j$ be the estimated compute time of access $j$, $L$ the time at which the link becomes free and $R=C_w-\sum_{\mathcal P}s_u$ the streaming buffer. A streamed access $j$ is fetched as early as the link is free and the buffer has room, i.e.\ once earlier streamed units have finished computing and released enough bytes; it then computes when both the previous layer and its own fetch have finished:
\begin{align}
f_j &= \max(L,\ \rho_j) + t_{\mathrm{copy}}(s_j), & L &\leftarrow f_j,\\
b_j &= \max(e_{j-1},\ f_j), & e_j &= b_j + \hat c_j ,
\end{align}
where $\rho_j$ is the earliest time at which the buffer holds at most $R-s_j$ bytes of streamed units, and pinned accesses skip the fetch. The planner costs $O(|Q|)$ per evaluation.

\paragraph{Search and verification}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:scp} shortlists structured candidates (no pinning, prefix, frequency-ranked, knapsack-DP, evenly interleaved ``stride'' sets at five pinned fractions, and the lazy marginal-gain greedy set), scores them with $T_{\mathrm{plan}}$, refines the best by add/remove/local-swap search and a short simulated-annealing polish (about 1\,200 planner evaluations), and finally replays the shortlisted sets in the event engine for three iterations \emph{using the cost model's estimated layer times}, never the device's true times, and keeps the fastest replay. The replay removes planner approximations, such as ignoring buffer reuse across iterations, from the final decision. Planning takes 0.2--0.7~s for 160-unit models on one core and is performed once per operating point (or after calibration), not per token.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Stall-cost-aware pinning (SCP)}
\label{alg:scp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} unit sequence with sizes, cost model $\hat t$, link model $t_{\mathrm{copy}}$, capacity $C_w$
\State $\mathcal S \gets \{\emptyset\}\cup\{\textsc{Stride}(g): g\in\{.3,.45,.6,.75,.9\}\}\cup\{\textsc{Prefix},\textsc{Freq},\textsc{KnapsackDP},\textsc{Greedy}\}$ \Comment{feasible sets only}
\State $\mathcal P^\ast \gets \arg\min_{\mathcal P\in\mathcal S}\, T_{\mathrm{plan}}(\mathcal P)$
\State $\mathcal P^\ast \gets \textsc{LocalSearch}(\mathcal P^\ast)$ \Comment{add / remove / swap within $\pm3$ positions}
\State $\mathcal P^\ast \gets \textsc{Anneal}(\mathcal P^\ast,\ 1200\text{ evaluations})$
\State $\mathcal S \gets \mathcal S\cup\{\mathcal P^\ast\}$
\For{$\mathcal P\in\mathcal S$}
  \State $\tau(\mathcal P) \gets$ replay 3 iterations in the event engine with estimated layer times
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} $\arg\min_{\mathcal P\in\mathcal S}\tau(\mathcal P)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Why placement matters}
Consider $N$ equal units with compute $c$ and fetch time $f>c$, a streaming buffer holding $m\ge 2$ units, and $P$ pinned units. If the pinned units form a contiguous prefix, the link can only run $m-1$ units ahead while they compute, and idles for the rest:
\begin{align}
T_{\mathrm{prefix}}&\approx (N-P)\,f + \max\{0,\ P\,c-(m-1)f\},\nonumber\\
T_{\mathrm{interleaved}}&\approx \max\{(N-P)\,f,\ N\,c\}.
\label{eq:placement}
\end{align}
The penalty vanishes when $c\ll f$ (decode), and is largest when compute and transfer are comparable. Frequency-ranked and prefix pinning, which break ties by sequence position, generate exactly the contiguous case; Section~\ref{sec:res-pins} measures the link utilisation they lose.

\paragraph{Transient layers and eviction}
Units that are not pinned are evicted by next-use order (furthest next use first), which is optimal for the known order \cite{belady1966study}; the admission rule of Algorithm~\ref{alg:prefetch} prevents a prefetch from displacing a nearer use. On a cyclic scan with equal frequencies, LRU, FIFO and LFU all evict the layer that will be needed soonest and retain nothing (Section~\ref{sec:res-reuse}).

\section{Evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Simulator}
\label{sec:sim}

The simulator (\texttt{camp\_sim}, released with this paper) is an event-driven model of one GPU, one copy engine and an HBM weight cache. It contains no policy: prefetch, victim selection and pinning are plug-ins. The compute stream executes accesses strictly in order; each access first issues its demand fetch, then lets the policy issue lookahead, then waits for the weights. Copy completions and layer completions are events, and policies are re-invoked at both. Noise is drawn from a generator keyed by (seed, event identity), and the bandwidth drift is indexed by simulated time, so different policies run with the same seed see the same environment for the same copy or layer (common random numbers); policy comparisons use paired seeds. All experiments use ten seeds unless stated; we report means and 95\% confidence half-widths ($t$-distribution; at most \nCiMaxPct\% of the mean for \camp-v2 in Table~\ref{tab:main}). Layer weights are never materialised: the simulator tracks bytes, not tensors.

Three things are checked independently of the experiments. (i) the test suite (\texttt{tests/test\_engine.py}) compares the deterministic engine with closed-form pipeline times (demand paging $N(f+c)$; prefetch with a ring buffer $N\max(f,c)$; pinned layers $(N-P)f$; compute-bound $Nc$; non-uniform unit sizes; next-use retention on a cyclic scan; noise reproducibility), which agree to within 2\%. (ii) The analytic planner agrees with the engine to a mean absolute error of \nPlannerMape\% (maximum \nPlannerMax\%) over \nPlannerCount{} random model/workload/cache/link configurations (Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}). (iii) The link and overlap logic is compared with a real GPU (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

\subsection{Hardware check of the link and overlap logic}
\label{sec:validation}

The simulator's novel parts are the copy path and the overlap of transfers with compute. We check them on a real GPU with CUDA streams and events (\texttt{validation/hw\_validate.py}, run on a Tesla T4, PCIe Gen3 x16, CUDA 13). \emph{This is a PCIe-attached GPU reading page-locked host DRAM: no CXL device was measured}, and the check says nothing about CXL device latency or bandwidth, which we take from the literature. Twenty-four weight tensors of 64~MiB live in page-locked host memory, standing in for the pool; layer $u$ computes a GEMM of $T$ tokens against its tensor. The loop supports a configurable prefetch depth (ring of device slots, events for slot reuse and copy completion) and a number of tensors pinned in HBM. We measure, \emph{in the same loop}, (a) a compute-only iteration (all tensors resident) for each $T$, (b) a copy-only iteration, and (c) \nHwN{} composite configurations (five $T$ values $\times$ six combinations of depth and pinned count), three repetitions each. The simulator is then composed from the separately measured compute time per unit and the fitted copy cost (\nHwPinnedBw~GB/s, \nHwLatUs~$\mu$s) and asked to predict the composites. No parameter is fitted to the composites.

Table~\ref{tab:hw} and Figure~\ref{fig:hw} give the result, and it is mixed. Across the \nHwN{} configurations the mean absolute error is \nHwMape\% (median \nHwMedian\%, maximum \nHwMax\%). In the link-bound regime (prefetch depth $\ge1$, $T\le1024$, no pinned tensors; nine configurations) the error is \nHwLinkBoundMape\% on average and at most \nHwLinkBoundMax\%, which supports the copy path, queueing and overlap logic that every later result depends on. The error grows where compute time per unit is comparable to or larger than copy time, and its sign differs between regimes: under demand paging the simulator \nHwDemandSign-predicts by up to \nHwDemandMax\% (the measured serial loop is faster than the sum of the separately measured copy and compute times), and in overlapped, compute-bound configurations ($T\ge2048$) it under-predicts by \nHwBigMean\% on average (compute in the composite loop is slower than in the compute-only loop). We did not isolate the cause; candidate explanations such as GPU power or clock state differing between back-to-back and interleaved execution, or driver-level interaction between copies and kernels, are untested. Configurations with pinned tensors have a mean error of \nHwPinnedMape\%. Two limits of this check should be stated plainly. First, a closed-form expression with the same measured inputs, $\max(n_s t_{\mathrm{copy}},N t_{\mathrm{comp}})$ for overlapped runs and $n_s t_{\mathrm{copy}}+N t_{\mathrm{comp}}$ for demand paging, has a mean error of \nHwCfMape\% over the same configurations, so on this simple loop the simulator is no more accurate than algebra; the check validates its inputs and overlap semantics, not its additional machinery (non-uniform units, queueing, noise), which is covered by the tests above. Second, the loop uses a fixed-depth ring, not \camp's work-conserving policy, so the policy itself is not exercised on hardware. Pageable (staged) copies reached \nHwRatio{} of the pinned bandwidth (\nHwPageableBw{} versus \nHwPinnedBw~GB/s), which implies a staging-hop rate of \nHwStaging~GB/s in the serial two-hop model.

\subsection{Workloads, baselines and metrics}
\label{sec:eval-setup}

Workloads use the models of Table~\ref{tab:models}. Large-model configurations (``main'') are Llama-3-70B and Llama-3-405B with the HBM left after the KV cache of the workload as weight cache: decode with $B=16$ sequences and 2k-token contexts (KV capacity reserved for 4k), mixed iterations with 32 decode tokens plus a 512-token prefill chunk, and prefill of four 2k- or 4k-token prompts. ``Constrained'' configurations give a 13B or 9B model 40\% of its weight bytes as cache, as on a GPU shared with other tenants. Non-uniform reuse is evaluated with Gemma-2-9B (tied embedding and head), speculative decoding (13B target, 1B draft, $k=4$ draft steps per verification) and a 24+24-layer encoder--decoder processing 64 output tokens per request.

Baselines are given the same cache capacity and link, and the same tuning effort that \camp{} does not need:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Demand+LRU}: no prefetch (the behaviour of an unmanaged mapping).
\item \textbf{Static-$k$}: fixed lookahead $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$ chosen per configuration, LRU, no pinning. \textbf{Reactive} (lookahead grows on observed stalls, TMO-like \cite{tmo}) and \textbf{Aggressive-10} (fixed deep lookahead, Limoncello-like \cite{limoncello}) behave as Static-$k$ without pins (within \nReaOverSkMax\%) and are listed only in \texttt{results/e2\_main.json}.
\item \textbf{FlexGen-style}: a uniform fraction of \emph{every} layer stays resident, the rest is streamed through $n$ buffers of the largest unit with lookahead $k$, FIFO eviction \cite{sheng2023flexgen}; the fraction follows from $n$, and $(n,k)$ is chosen per configuration by simulation from ten candidates (a stand-in for FlexGen's policy search). The split is modelled at fractional granularity, finer than \camp's whole units.
\item \textbf{Hot/Cold}: resident set chosen by access frequency, ties broken at random, up to capacity minus one streaming slot (``equal budget''), streaming with tuned lookahead (PowerInfer-style partitioning \cite{song2024powerinfer} adapted to streaming).
\item \textbf{Stride}: evenly interleaved resident set at equal budget with \camp's prefetcher; this isolates the value of interleaving without the planner.
\item \textbf{\camp-v1}: the earlier formulation, with lookahead gated by the compute estimate and frequency pinning at the conventional two-slot reserve, and \textbf{\camp-v2} (this paper), with the cost model calibrated after one probe iteration and SCP with dry-run verification; variants with the generic (uncalibrated) and the exact device model are reported where relevant.
\end{itemize}
These are reimplementations of the \emph{mechanisms} in the common simulator, not ports of the original systems. Section~\ref{sec:res-attrib} adds one ingredient at a time to a conventional hot/cold baseline to show which differences matter.

The metric is the steady-state iteration latency (the first iteration is a warm-up and excluded). \emph{Speed-up vs.\ demand paging} is the demand-paging latency divided by \camp-v2's; \emph{speed-up vs.\ best external} is the best of Demand, Static-$k$, FlexGen-style, Hot/Cold and \camp-v1 divided by \camp-v2's (so values below one mean \camp{} is slower); \emph{det./LB} is the noise-free \camp-v2 latency divided by $\max(T_{\mathrm{LB}}, \sum_u t_u)$ with the bound of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lb}).

\subsection{Regime map: when does streaming from CXL pay off?}
\label{sec:res-regime}

Figure~\ref{fig:regime} gives the latency of \camp-managed streaming relative to a GPU with all weights resident, for Llama-3-70B with 40\% of the weights cached, as a function of tokens per iteration and of $\bwlink$. At 18~GB/s the slowdown is \nRegSlowOneEighteen$\times$ for one token, \nRegSlowSixtyFourEighteen$\times$ at 64 tokens, \nRegSlowTwoFiftySixEighteen$\times$ at 256, \nRegSlowKiloEighteen$\times$ at 1\,024, \nRegSlowFourKEighteen$\times$ at 4\,096, \nRegSlowSixteenKEighteen$\times$ at 16\,384 and \nRegSlowSixtyFiveKEighteen$\times$ at 65\,536. Prefill of long prompts and very large batches can therefore run from CXL-resident weights at close to resident speed, so capacity can be traded for little latency. Interactive decode cannot: with at most 256 tokens per iteration the slowdown is \nRegSlowTwoFiftySixEighteen--\nRegSlowOneEighteen$\times$ at 18~GB/s, and the only lever is to move fewer bytes. Neither prefetching nor cleverer replacement changes this; a larger cache, a faster link, quantisation (Section~\ref{sec:res-alt}) or compression \cite{xie2025trace} do. Across the whole map \camp{} is \nRegCampOverLbMin--\nRegCampOverLbMax$\times$ the lower bound (median \nRegCampOverLbMed) and \nRegDemandOverCampMin--\nRegDemandOverCampMax$\times$ faster than demand paging.

\subsection{Main comparison}
\label{sec:res-main}

Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:constrained} and Figure~\ref{fig:main} compare the policies in \nMainRows{} rows (\nMainConfigs{} workloads at two link bandwidths). Four observations.

\emph{\camp-v2 is close to the lower bound everywhere.} Without noise its latency is \nDetLbMin--\nDetLbMax$\times$ the bound of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lb}) (median \nDetLbMed), and with the default noise \nNoisyLbMin--\nNoisyLbMax$\times$ (values just below one are noise around a deterministic bound). The largest gaps are in the balanced 4k-token prefill configurations at 18~GB/s.

\emph{Against demand paging the gain is large; against the strongest baseline it is not.} \camp-v2 is \nDemOverCampMin--\nDemOverCampMax$\times$ faster than demand paging (median \nDemOverCampMed; \nConstrainedDemOverCampMin--\nConstrainedDemOverCampMax$\times$ in the constrained rows), \nSkOverCampMin--\nSkOverCampMax$\times$ faster than the best fixed-lookahead prefetcher, up to \nHcOverCampMax$\times$ faster than the equal-budget Hot/Cold baseline and up to \nVOneOverCampMax$\times$ faster than \camp-v1. The best of the five external baselines, which on the large models is the tuned FlexGen-style split, is within 1\% of \camp-v2 in \nTieExt{} of \nMainRows{} rows; \camp-v2 is faster by up to \nExtOverCampMaxPct\% in \nWinExt{} rows (both Gemma-2) and slower by up to \nWorstLossExt\% in \nLoseExt{} rows (13B and 70B prefill at 18~GB/s). On the 70B and 405B configurations alone, \camp-v2 is within \nMainExtOverCampMin--\nMainExtOverCampMax$\times$ of the best external baseline, i.e.\ it ties it. A first version of the FlexGen-style baseline fixed two buffers and a lookahead of one or two layers and was markedly slower than \camp{} in several rows; tuning its buffer count and lookahead removed most of the gap, and we report the tuned baseline.

\emph{Interleaving, not the planner, explains most of the gain over a conventional baseline.} The stride baseline, which pins evenly spaced units and uses \camp's prefetcher but has no planner, is slower than \camp-v2 by more than 1\% in \nStLoses{} of \nMainRows{} rows and by up to \nStLoseMaxPct\%. The remaining difference between stride pins and SCP is largest in balanced prefill at 18~GB/s (Section~\ref{sec:res-attrib}).

\emph{Calibration is not decisive.} Replacing the calibrated cost model by the generic one changes \camp-v2's latency by at most \nUncalMaxPct\%, and by the exact device model by at most \nExactMaxPct\% (Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}).

For Gemma-2-9B, \camp-v2 is \nHetGemmaFg$\times$ faster than the tuned FlexGen-style split and \nHetGemmaFreq$\times$ faster than frequency pinning at equal budget; we have not isolated the cause, but the likely reason is that the 1.8~GB tied embedding/head table dominates the unit sizes, so a uniform fractional split must reserve streaming buffers sized by it, whereas SCP can pin the table itself.

Figure~\ref{fig:pins} compares pin-selection rules with the prefetcher fixed (\camp's controller, next-use eviction): no pinning, prefix, frequency-ranked (conventional two-slot reserve, and equal budget with random tie-break), interleaved stride, the exact knapsack of Eq.~(\ref{eq:knap}), the marginal-gain greedy set, and SCP. \emph{SCP is robust rather than uniformly best.} Against the best of the six simpler rules in each configuration, SCP lost at most \nScpRegretMaxPct\% and gained more than 1\% in \nScpBeatsSimple{} of \nPinCfg{} configurations, by up to \nScpGainOverBestSimpleMax\%. No single simple rule is best across the configurations (stride in balanced prefill, frequency in encoder--decoder inference, none in some compute-bound cases); SCP selects among them, and finds sets outside them, without being told which applies.

\emph{Optimality.} On an 18-unit model, where all $2^{18}$ pin sets can be enumerated, SCP is on average \nGapScpMean\% (maximum \nGapScpMax\%) above the exhaustive optimum of the planner objective over \nGapCount{} cases, versus up to \nGapStrideMax\% for stride and \nGapFreqMax\% for frequency ranking (Table~\ref{tab:gap}); replayed in the event engine the maximum gap of SCP is \nGapSimMax\%, which marks the planner's approximation error that the dry-run verification reduces but does not remove. SCP is a heuristic with no optimality guarantee.

\subsection{How much does the compute estimate matter?}
\label{sec:res-cost}

Table~\ref{tab:costmodel} and Fig.~\ref{fig:cost} address how $\hat t_u$ is obtained and what happens when it is wrong. The generic roofline estimate has a mean absolute error of \nCmMapeAGen\% (13B prefill), \nCmMapeBGen\% (70B prefill) and \nCmMapeCGen\% (70B decode) against the simulated device; one probe iteration reduces this to \nCmMapeACal, \nCmMapeBCal{} and \nCmMapeCCal\%. Yet the latency of \camp-v2 with SCP planned from the generic, the calibrated or the exact model differs by at most \nCmSpreadB\% in these workloads, whereas removing pinning altogether costs \nCmNoPinOverA--\nCmNoPinOverB$\times$. Prefetching is insensitive to the estimate (the work-conserving controller and the earlier compute-gated one give identical latencies). The estimate can mislead the pinning stage if it is \emph{systematically biased}: with a static estimator whose constants are off by factors of 0.25 to 4, SCP's latency is up to \nCmBiasWorstA\% (13B prefill) and \nCmBiasWorstB\% (70B prefill) above the best; after online calibration the worst case over the bias range is \nCmBiasOnlineWorstA\% and \nCmBiasOnlineWorstB\%. Decode is insensitive throughout (at most \nCmBiasWorstC\%) because it is link-bound.

A tighter test is a ground truth that the estimator cannot represent. Table~\ref{tab:shape} makes the simulated GEMM efficiency ramp with arithmetic intensity, which the estimator ignores. The generic estimate then has a mean error of up to \nShapeMapeGeneric\%; calibrating on the evaluated shape reduces it to 0.1\%, and calibrating on a different shape (decode probe, prefill evaluation or vice versa) leaves up to \nShapeMapeCross\%, yet the latency of the resulting plan differs from the oracle plan by at most \nShapeLatSpreadPct\%. In summary, \camp{} needs the compute estimate only for pinning, only in the balanced regime, and an exponential moving average over one iteration of observed layer times is enough; we do not consider the cost model a central contribution.

\paragraph{Batching}
Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} shows throughput for Llama-3-70B. The cached share of the weights shrinks from \nKvCacheFirstPct\% at $B=1$ to \nKvCacheLastPct\% at $B=\nKvMaxB$, and $B=\nKvInfeasB$ no longer fits at all. Streaming amortises weight traffic over the batch, but the KV cache that a large batch needs competes with the weights for HBM.

\subsection{Alternatives to streaming}
\label{sec:res-alt}

Streaming from CXL competes with other ways to run a model that does not fit. Table~\ref{tab:alt} compares it with weight quantisation, pinned host DRAM and two GPUs (all other columns use \camp-v2). Quantisation is the strongest alternative where its accuracy cost is acceptable: FP8/INT8 weights make 70B decode \nAltSeventyEightDecRatio$\times$ faster over the same 18~GB/s link (\nAltSeventyEightDec{} versus \nAltSeventyFpDec~ms) and 70B prefill fits one GPU, and INT4 weights fit entirely; for 405B even INT4 weights (\nAltFourOhFiveFourWeights~GB) need streaming and decode takes \nAltFourOhFiveFourDec~s. Pinned host DRAM at 52~GB/s is the same mechanism over a faster link (70B decode \nAltSeventyFpHostDec~ms versus \nAltSeventyFpDec~ms at 18~GB/s and \nAltSeventyFpCxlFortyFiveDec~ms at 45~GB/s). For FP16 70B prefill, streaming takes \nAltSeventyFpPre~ms at 18~GB/s against an optimistic \nAltSeventyTwoGpuPre~ms on two GPUs (perfect scaling, no communication). Streaming from CXL is therefore attractive when quantisation is not acceptable, when the model exceeds the GPU count available, or when the iteration is compute-dense; it does not compete with a second GPU on latency.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the results mean for CXL-backed inference}
With an effective bandwidth of 10--20~GB/s per expander, streaming weights from CXL is practical for compute-dense iterations (long-prompt prefill, very large batches) and impractical for interactive decode, where the slowdown against resident weights is tens of times and prefetching cannot help because the iteration is link-bound. For decode the achievable gains come from the number of resident bytes and their placement, and from raising $\bwlink$ (more expanders, page-locked DMA, compression, quantisation). Once placement is interleaved, policy sophistication buys little on dense models with uniform layers: a tuned FlexGen-style split is within a few per cent of \camp. What \camp{} adds is (i) not needing that tuning, (ii) a verified plan whose quality is known against a lower bound, and (iii) correct handling of non-uniform unit sizes and reuse, where uniform splits and stride rules lose up to a factor of two. Whether (i)--(iii) justify a more complex runtime is a deployment decision; our results say the gain is modest for dense models and large for non-uniform ones.

\paragraph{Cost of re-planning}
Changing the resident set requires moving up to the pinned bytes once. At 18~GB/s that is \nRepinSeventyDecS~s for the \nRepinSeventyDecGb~GB pinned for 70B decode, \nRepinSeventyPreS~s for the \nRepinSeventyPreGb~GB pinned for 70B prefill, and \nRepinThirteenDecS~s for \nRepinThirteenDecGb~GB for 13B decode. Relative to running the same workload without pinning, the move pays for itself after about \nRepinSeventyDecIters, \nRepinSeventyPreIters{} and \nRepinThirteenDecIters{} iterations respectively, so re-planning is worthwhile only when the operating point persists for more than a few iterations. The move is not charged in the experiments.

\paragraph{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{No CXL hardware.} The CXL link is simulated; its bandwidth and fixed costs are taken from published measurements \cite{yoon2025tract,jang2026itme} and swept over a wide range, and the link and overlap logic is checked on a PCIe-attached T4 with pinned host DRAM in place of a CXL pool. We did not measure a real expander, a PCIe Gen5 GPU or an H100; the compute times of the H100-class device come from a roofline model with efficiencies that we did not measure.
\item \emph{Partly unexplained hardware errors.} The check on the T4 shows errors of up to \nHwMax\% where compute and copy are comparable, with a sign that differs between regimes, and a closed-form expression is as accurate as the simulator there. We have not isolated the cause. These are exactly the regimes in which placement matters, so the quantitative gains in the balanced regime carry this uncertainty.
\item \emph{Baselines are reimplementations} of mechanisms, not ports of FlexGen, PowerInfer, TMO or Limoncello. The FlexGen-style baseline is tuned per configuration by simulation and modelled at fractional layer granularity, which is more favourable to it than whole-unit placement; conversely we did not implement FlexGen's full LP, which also places KV cache and activations. Reusing the same simulator for all policies removes implementation noise but also shares any modelling error among them.
\item \emph{Scope of the models.} We evaluate dense transformers with a static order. Mixture-of-Experts routing makes the access order data-dependent; hot/cold partitioning by observed frequency \cite{song2024powerinfer,xue2024moeinfinity} is the natural baseline and an online variant of SCP is future work.
\item \emph{Single GPU, single tenant.} Multi-GPU sharing of one CXL pool, link contention between tenants, CXL switches and contention with host memory traffic are not modelled.
\item \emph{The KV cache stays in HBM.} Offloading it to CXL \cite{yoon2025tract,lee2024infinigen} competes for the same link, and Section~\ref{sec:res-scale} shows how strongly the KV cache already competes with the weights for HBM.
\item \emph{SCP is a heuristic.} It is within \nGapScpMax\% of the exhaustive optimum of its own objective on an 18-unit model, but there is no guarantee for larger models, and in the event engine the set it selects is up to \nGapSimMax\% slower than the planner-optimal set; the dry-run replay mitigates but does not remove this.
\item \emph{Whole-unit granularity.} \camp{} places whole attention or feed-forward blocks. With a high resident share (13B decode, 86\% resident) a sub-layer split is better by \nThirteenDecCampOverFg$\times$ (Section~\ref{sec:res-scale}); applying SCP to tensor-level units is a straightforward extension that we did not evaluate.
\item \emph{Heterogeneous workloads are synthetic where noted.} The encoder--decoder and speculative-decoding workloads are constructed from model-card configurations, not traces of a deployed system.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied LLM inference with weights streamed from CXL expanders through a host-mediated DMA path. A closed-form threshold and a regime map show when streaming is nearly free (above about 16k tokens per iteration) and when it is tens of times slower (decode). Which layers are resident matters less than where they sit: contiguous resident sets idle the link when compute and transfer are comparable, interleaved ones do not, and a per-layer fractional split in the style of FlexGen is already interleaved. \camp{}, which combines a work-conserving prefetcher with stall-cost-aware pinning, stays within \nDetLbMed--\nDetLbMax$\times$ of an implementation-independent lower bound in simulation, is up to \nDemOverCampMax$\times$ faster than demand paging, ties the best baseline on dense uniform models and is ahead where unit sizes or reuse are non-uniform; the compute estimate and the LFU/LRU distinction, often presented as central, matter little. The link and overlap logic of the simulator was checked on a real GPU with host memory standing in for the pool, with mixed accuracy that we report as is. The main open items are measurement on real CXL hardware and PCIe Gen5 GPUs, tensor-level pinning, online variants for Mixture-of-Experts, and multi-tenant sharing of the pool.
