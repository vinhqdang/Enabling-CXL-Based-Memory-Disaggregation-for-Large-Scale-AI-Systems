\title{CAMP: Graph-Aware Prefetching and Pinning for LLM Inference over Host-Mediated CXL Memory}

\author[buv]{Quang-Vinh Dang\corref{cor1}}
\ead{vinh.dq4@buv.edu.vn}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author. ORCID: 0000-0002-3877-8024}
\address[buv]{British University Vietnam, Hung Yen, Vietnam}

\begin{abstract}
Large language models whose weights exceed GPU memory can be served by keeping part of the weights in CXL-attached memory and streaming them into HBM. A CXL Type-3 expander is not addressable by the GPU, so every transfer is a host-initiated DMA limited by the slower of the CXL device and the PCIe link, which published prototypes put at roughly 10 to 20 GB/s. We present \camp, a runtime policy that exploits the static layer order of inference through three components: an online-calibrated roofline cost model that needs no offline profiling, a work-conserving prefetcher with next-use admission, and stall-cost-aware pinning (SCP), which scores candidate sets of resident layers with a pipeline planner and verifies them in a dry run. We evaluate with an event-driven simulator whose copy path, compute model and noise follow measured data; its pipeline logic is checked against a real GPU on 30 streaming configurations (7.6\% mean error overall, 4.0\% when link-bound). On Llama-2, Llama-3 and Gemma-2 models, for prefill, decode and mixed batches, \camp{} stays within 6\% of a lower bound and is up to 1.47 times faster than the best static baseline and 2.9 times faster than demand paging. The gain comes from where layers are pinned, not how often they are reused: contiguous pinning idles the link, and a frequency rule can lose to random pinning. Streaming from CXL is nearly free only above about 16k tokens per iteration; small-batch decode is 20 to 80 times slower than resident weights.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The weights of current large language models no longer fit in the memory of a single accelerator: Llama-3-70B needs 141~GB in FP16 and Llama-3.1-405B needs 812~GB, against 80~GB of HBM on a high-end GPU. The usual remedies are to shard the model over several GPUs, which multiplies the hardware bill, or to offload part of it to host DRAM, SSD or a remote tier and stream it back on demand \cite{sheng2023flexgen,rajbhandari2021infinity}. Compute Express Link (CXL) memory expanders add a new option: byte-addressable DDR capacity attached to the host over PCIe-class links, at lower cost per gigabyte than HBM and without the capacity ceiling of the DIMM slots \cite{pond2023,tpp2023}. The catch is bandwidth. Prototype Type-3 devices sustain on the order of 10--20~GB/s towards a GPU \cite{yoon2025tract,jang2026itme}, two orders of magnitude below HBM.

Two facts shape the problem and are easy to overlook when CXL memory is treated as a directly attached GPU memory tier. First, a CXL Type-3 expander is a CXL.mem endpoint behind the host root complex; a GPU cannot address it directly. Weights reach HBM through a host-initiated \texttt{cudaMemcpyAsync} from page-locked, CXL-backed host memory, so the achievable rate is set by the slower of the CXL device and the PCIe link, and every copy carries software and synchronisation costs \cite{yoon2025tract}. Second, the access order of transformer inference is static, but the compute available to hide a transfer is not: it scales with the number of tokens processed per iteration. Streaming a layer of $p$ parameters costs $2p$ bytes of link traffic and enables $2pT$ FLOPs for $T$ tokens, so a link of 18~GB/s hides the transfer only when $T$ is in the tens of thousands (Section~\ref{sec:model}). Prefill of long prompts and very large batches are in that regime; interactive decode is not.

This paper studies weight streaming over a host-mediated CXL path and proposes \camp{} (Content-Aware Memory Prefetching), a runtime policy that decides \emph{when} to fetch layers and \emph{which} layers stay resident in HBM. The policy is deliberately small, because the access order is known: it combines (i) an online-calibrated roofline cost model that predicts per-layer compute time without offline profiling, (ii) a work-conserving prefetcher that admits a fetch only if it displaces something needed later, and (iii) \emph{stall-cost-aware pinning} (SCP), which chooses the resident set by minimising a predicted iteration time rather than by ranking layers by reuse frequency. We are explicit about what is and is not new. Hot/cold partitioning by frequency \cite{song2024powerinfer}, static placement with overlapped I/O \cite{sheng2023flexgen} and layer-wise DMA prefetching from CXL-attached memory \cite{jang2026itme} exist; we implement them as baselines. What we add is a quantified account of when streaming from CXL pays off, a pin-selection rule that accounts for pipeline placement, and a validation of the simulator's pipeline machinery on a real GPU.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Path model and regime analysis (Sections~\ref{sec:model}, \ref{sec:res-regime}).} A host-mediated transfer model with explicit initiator, bandwidth bound, per-copy and synchronisation costs and a staged (bounce-buffer) variant; a closed-form arithmetic-intensity threshold; and a regime map showing that CAMP-managed streaming is within 1.14$\times$ of resident weights only above roughly 16k tokens per iteration (18~GB/s), while decode with at most 256 tokens per iteration is 66--79$\times$ slower.
\item \textbf{The CAMP policy (Section~\ref{sec:camp}).} A runtime cost model that is calibrated online from observed layer durations (23\% generic roofline error becomes 0.5--2.7\% after one probe iteration); a work-conserving prefetcher whose decisions do not depend on the compute estimate; and SCP, a plan-and-verify pin selection whose objective, constraints and relation to the 0--1 knapsack are stated exactly.
\item \textbf{Where pinning gains come from (Sections~\ref{sec:res-pins}, \ref{sec:res-reuse}).} In link-bound decode every sensible pin rule is within 3\% of the others; the benefit of SCP appears when compute and transfer are comparable, where contiguous (prefix or frequency-ranked) pinning leaves the link idle 12--21\% of the time and can be slower than random pinning. LFU is indistinguishable from LRU on cyclic uniform traces, so a frequency-based eviction policy provides no protection there.
\item \textbf{Evaluation methodology (Sections~\ref{sec:method}, \ref{sec:results}).} An event-driven simulator whose pipeline logic is validated against a real GPU on 30 streaming configurations, an implementation-independent lower bound, ten seeds with common random numbers, and evaluation on Llama-2/3 and Gemma-2 configurations from 9B to 405B parameters in prefill, decode and mixed-batch iterations. \camp{} is within 6\% of the lower bound in all 16 main configurations and up to 1.47$\times$ faster than the best static baseline.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope} We target dense transformer models whose layer order is static. We do not evaluate Mixture-of-Experts routing, multi-GPU or multi-tenant sharing of a CXL pool, KV-cache placement in CXL, or CXL switches. No CXL 3.x hardware was available: the CXL link is simulated with parameters anchored in published measurements, and the hardware validation (Section~\ref{sec:validation}) uses pinned host memory on a PCIe-attached GPU as a stand-in for the pool.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reaching CXL memory from a GPU}
CXL expanders have been studied mainly as a CPU-side capacity tier: Pond \cite{pond2023} and TPP \cite{tpp2023} place or migrate pages between local DRAM and CXL memory, and DirectCXL \cite{directcxl2022} and the characterisations of Melody \cite{melody2025} and Sun et al.\ \cite{demystifying2023} report latencies of roughly 140--410~ns and device-dependent bandwidth. Work on GPUs is more recent and confirms the host-mediated nature of the path. TraCT \cite{yoon2025tract} reports that a plain \texttt{cudaMemcpy} from a CXL-mapped region goes through a bounce buffer in host DRAM and must be avoided by page-locking the region so that the DMA engine reads the device directly; it measures 10.1~GB/s and 640~ns for a second-generation expander. ITME \cite{jang2026itme} prefetches model weights layer by layer from a CXL-hybrid memory into GPU memory with a DMA pipeline and reports about 18~GB/s for the prototype. DAK \cite{lin2026dak} instead lets the GPU read remote memory directly through the Tensor Memory Accelerator on NVLink-C2C and PCIe systems, and cache-coherent GPU--CPU links such as GH200 expose coherent memory to the GPU \cite{wang2024hitchhiker}; these are different access paths from the one we model. Compression raises the effective bandwidth of a CXL link \cite{xie2025trace} and is orthogonal to the policy studied here. Full-system CXL simulators with silicon validation, such as CXL-DMSim \cite{wang2025cxldmsim}, model the device and host side in far more detail than we do but, to our knowledge, do not model GPU kernels.

\paragraph{Weight offloading for LLM inference}
FlexGen \cite{sheng2023flexgen} formulates the placement of weights, activations and KV cache over GPU, CPU and disk as a linear program and overlaps I/O with compute for throughput-oriented generation; ZeRO-Inference/Infinity \cite{rajbhandari2021infinity} stream partitioned weights from CPU and NVMe. PowerInfer \cite{song2024powerinfer} keeps frequently activated (``hot'') neurons on the GPU and computes the rest on the CPU, exploiting activation sparsity rather than streaming weights. Serving systems batch iteration by iteration \cite{yu2022orca,kwon2023vllm}, and chunked prefill \cite{agrawal2024sarathi} mixes prompt chunks with decode tokens, which is what makes the arithmetic intensity of an iteration a tunable quantity. KV-cache offloading \cite{lee2024infinigen,qin2025mooncake} and prefill/decode disaggregation \cite{zhong2024distserve} address different data and are complementary.

\paragraph{Prefetching and replacement under a known access order}
Prefetch and tiering policies for general workloads infer behaviour from pressure or access samples \cite{tmo,limoncello}. When the future order is known, Belady's optimal replacement \cite{belady1966study} gives the offline reference; graph-aware memory planners exploit it for activation memory in training \cite{chen2024magis,zhao2025memo}. For repeated cyclic scans of a model, recency-based replacement fails by construction, which is the behaviour that pinning is meant to break.

\paragraph{Positioning}
Table~\ref{tab:positioning} summarises the closest systems. The closest in spirit are FlexGen and ITME, which also overlap weight transfers with compute, and PowerInfer, which also partitions by frequency. \camp{} differs in operating at layer granularity over a host-mediated CXL path with an explicit transfer model, in choosing the resident set by a pipeline-aware objective and a verification step instead of a frequency or prefix rule, and in deriving the compute estimate it needs at runtime. We do not claim that frequency-based hot/cold partitioning is inferior in general: in link-bound decode it is within 3\% of SCP (Section~\ref{sec:res-pins}).

\section{System model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Host-mediated CXL-to-GPU path}
\label{sec:path}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the path that the simulator models. The CXL Type-3 expander is exposed to the operating system as a CPU-less NUMA node. The \camp{} runtime, a host thread, page-locks the CXL-backed region holding the weights and issues one \texttt{cudaMemcpyAsync} per layer unit on a dedicated copy stream; the GPU copy engine then reads the data through the root complex, which forwards the reads to the expander using CXL.mem, and writes it into the HBM slot of the target layer. Kernels of the consuming layer wait on a CUDA event recorded after the copy. The initiator is therefore the host, the data crosses two links in series (CXL, then PCIe), and the sustained rate is bounded by the slower of the two:
\begin{equation}
\bwlink = \eta\,\min(BW_{\mathrm{CXL}},\,BW_{\mathrm{PCIe}}),
\qquad
t_{\mathrm{copy}}(s) = t_0 + \Big\lceil \tfrac{s}{c}\Big\rceil t_c + t_{\mathrm{sync}} + \frac{s}{\bwlink},
\label{eq:copy}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the transfer size, $c$ the DMA chunk size, $t_0$ the per-copy API, driver and first-byte cost, $t_c$ the per-chunk descriptor cost and $t_{\mathrm{sync}}$ the event synchronisation cost. If the region is \emph{not} page-locked, the driver stages data through a bounce buffer in host DRAM; the CPU copy and the DMA are then serial and $\bwlink = (1/BW + 1/BW_{\mathrm{stage}})^{-1}$ (``staged'' mode). On a Tesla T4 we measured 12.4~GB/s for pinned and 4.6~GB/s for pageable copies, a ratio of 0.37 (Section~\ref{sec:validation}), consistent with the bounce-buffer penalty reported for CXL regions \cite{yoon2025tract}.

The simulator additionally includes (i) a single non-preemptive copy engine that serves demand fetches before queued prefetches, (ii) log-normal noise on every copy and a slow autoregressive drift of the link bandwidth that models interference from other traffic, and (iii) a fixed host sampling gap between decode iterations during which the link keeps working. It does \emph{not} model CXL switches or pooling across hosts, interleaving of several expanders (this is folded into $\bwlink$, which we sweep from 8 to 64~GB/s), GPU-initiated direct access to remote memory, cache-coherence traffic, or contention with host CPU memory traffic. Table~\ref{tab:platform} lists the parameters and their basis.

\subsection{Layer units and iteration traces}
\label{sec:units}

A model is decomposed into \emph{units}, the granularity at which weights are fetched and pinned: one attention block (QKV and output projections and norms), one feed-forward block, the embedding table and the output head (tied models share one unit). Table~\ref{tab:models} lists the configurations. An \emph{iteration} is one pass over the units: a prefill pass over $B$ prompts of $S$ tokens, a decode step for $B$ sequences with context length $C$, or a mixed iteration in which a prefill chunk piggybacks on decode tokens. The embedding access is a gather of $T$ rows rather than a full-table fetch. The KV cache and workspace stay in HBM and reduce the capacity $C_w$ available for weights: for Llama-3-70B with $B{=}16$ sequences of up to 4k tokens, the KV cache takes 21.5~GB, leaving $C_w=54.5$~GB (39\% of the weights).

\subsection{Compute model and regimes}
\label{sec:compute}

The execution time of a unit on the simulated GPU follows a roofline model \cite{williams2009roofline} per sub-operation:
\begin{equation}
t = \max\!\Big(\frac{F_g}{P\,\eta_g},\,\frac{B_g}{W\,\eta_m}\Big) + \max\!\Big(\frac{F_a}{P\,\eta_a},\,\frac{B_a}{W\,\eta_m}\Big) + n_k\,t_{\mathrm{launch}},
\label{eq:roofline}
\end{equation}
where $F_g,B_g$ are the FLOPs and HBM bytes of the projections, $F_a,B_a$ those of the attention core (including the KV cache read), $P$ the peak FLOP rate, $W$ the HBM bandwidth, $\eta$ the efficiencies and $n_k$ the number of kernels. This is the \emph{ground truth} of the simulated device; \camp{} sees only the features $(F_g,B_g,F_a,B_a,n_k)$ and must estimate $t$ (Section~\ref{sec:costmodel}).

Streaming a unit of $p$ parameters moves $b\,p$ bytes ($b=2$ for FP16) and enables $2pT$ FLOPs, so compute hides the copy when
\begin{equation}
T \;\ge\; T^\star = (1-\varphi)\,\frac{P\,\eta_g}{\bwlink}\cdot\frac{b}{2},
\label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
with $\varphi$ the fraction of weight bytes kept resident. For $P\eta_g = 613$~TFLOP/s, $\varphi=0.4$ and $\bwlink=18$ or 45~GB/s, $T^\star\approx 20\mathrm{k}$ or $8\mathrm{k}$ tokens per iteration. Decode with $B\le 512$ is therefore always link-bound, and the only lever is the number of bytes that cross the link per iteration. Section~\ref{sec:res-regime} confirms this threshold in the simulator.

\subsection{A lower bound}
\label{sec:method-lb}

An iteration cannot finish before its compute-only time $\sum_u t_u$, nor before the minimum number of streamed bytes has crossed the link. If any unit is streamed, one slot as large as the largest streamed unit must stay free, so with $W_{\mathrm{tot}}$ total weight bytes and cache capacity $C_w$ at least $W_{\mathrm{tot}}-(C_w-s)$ bytes are streamed, where $s$ is the size of the largest streamed unit (all larger units must then be pinned and fit in $C_w-s$). We take the smallest feasible $s$:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{LB}} = \max\Big(\sum_u t_u,\ \frac{\max\{0,\,W_{\mathrm{tot}}-C_w+s_{\min}\}}{\bwlink}\Big).
\label{eq:lb}
\end{equation}
The bound ignores per-copy overheads and pipeline fill, and is exact in the link-bound regime for uniform units. It is implementation-independent: no policy can beat it, and it gives each result an absolute reference.

\section{The CAMP policy}
\label{sec:camp}

\camp{} has three parts. All rely on the static unit order of the model (the ``graph''), none on offline profiling of the model or workload.

\subsection{Runtime cost model}
\label{sec:costmodel}

The prefetcher and the pinning stage need an estimate $\hat t_u$ of each unit's compute time for the \emph{current} batch descriptor $(B,S,C)$. \camp{} computes it from tensor shapes with the roofline of Eq.~(\ref{eq:roofline}), using generic constants for the device class (efficiencies $\hat\eta_g=0.50$, $\hat\eta_m=0.80$, $\hat\eta_a=0.35$) and the peak rates that the driver reports. These constants differ from the device's true efficiencies, so the estimate is calibrated online: for each unit kind $\kappa$ (attention, feed-forward, embedding, head) the runtime keeps a multiplicative correction updated by an exponential moving average of the ratio between observed and nominal duration,
\begin{equation}
\hat c_\kappa \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,\hat c_\kappa + \alpha\,\frac{t^{\mathrm{obs}}_u}{t^{\mathrm{nom}}_u},\qquad \hat t_u = \hat c_\kappa\, t^{\mathrm{nom}}_u,\quad \alpha=0.3 .
\label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}
The first iteration therefore runs with the generic estimate, and every later iteration, or any re-planning after the first probe iteration, uses the calibrated one. The current layer's own duration is \emph{not} needed before it completes: the estimate of unit $u$ is available from its features before it runs, and the observation after it runs only refines future estimates. We quantify the effect of estimator error in Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}.

\subsection{Work-conserving prefetching with next-use admission}
\label{sec:prefetch}

Because the unit order is static, the next use of every unit is known. Algorithm~\ref{alg:prefetch} issues prefetches whenever the link or a layer completes. A fetch of the unit needed at position $j$ is admitted if free capacity exists or if some resident, unpinned unit has its next use \emph{after} $j$ (Belady-style admission \cite{belady1966study}); otherwise the scan stops. The demand fetch of the layer about to run is always issued first, and prefetches cannot take capacity while a demand fetch is waiting for room. This controller is work-conserving: it never leaves the link idle if capacity allows another useful fetch. An earlier formulation limited the lookahead to the units whose cumulative transfer time fits in the estimated remaining compute of the current layer; in all our workloads that gate never binds, because completions re-trigger the controller, and results are identical with and without it (Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}). The estimate $\hat t_u$ is thus not on the critical path of prefetching; it matters for pinning.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Work-conserving prefetch controller (invoked at layer start, layer begin and every copy completion)}
\label{alg:prefetch}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} access order $Q$, current position $i$, cache with pinned set $\mathcal P$
\State $j \gets i+1$
\While{$j < |Q|$ \textbf{and} no demand fetch is blocked}
  \State $u \gets Q[j]$
  \If{$u$ is neither resident nor in flight}
    \If{free capacity $\ge$ size$(u)$}
      \State issue prefetch$(u)$
    \Else
      \State $v \gets \arg\max_{w\ \mathrm{resident},\,w\notin\mathcal P,\,w\ne Q[i]}\ \mathrm{nextuse}(w)$
      \If{nextuse$(v) > j$} evict $v$; issue prefetch$(u)$ \textbf{else} \textbf{break} \EndIf
    \EndIf
  \EndIf
  \State $j \gets j+1$
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Stall-cost-aware pinning}
\label{sec:scp}

\paragraph{Problem statement}
Let $U$ be the set of units with sizes $s_u$, $C_w$ the weight-cache capacity, $\mathcal P\subseteq U$ the pinned set and $T_{\mathrm{plan}}(\mathcal P)$ the predicted steady-state iteration time of the pipeline planner defined below. SCP solves
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathcal P\subseteq U}\ T_{\mathrm{plan}}(\mathcal P)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{u\in\mathcal P}s_u + \max_{v\in U\setminus\mathcal P}s_v \;\le\; C_w ,
\label{eq:scp}
\end{equation}
that is, the pinned units plus one streaming slot large enough for the largest streamed unit must fit. This is \emph{not} a 0--1 knapsack: $T_{\mathrm{plan}}$ is not additive over units and depends on where the pinned units sit in the sequence. The additive proxy
\begin{equation}
\max_{\mathcal P}\ \sum_{u\in\mathcal P}F(u)\,t_{\mathrm{copy}}(s_u)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{u\in\mathcal P}s_u \le C_w - s^\ast ,
\label{eq:knap}
\end{equation}
with $F(u)$ the number of accesses of $u$ per iteration, is a 0--1 knapsack whose benefit-per-byte is $F(u)\,t_{\mathrm{copy}}(s_u)/s_u\approx F(u)/\bwlink$. For uniform sizes it ranks units by $F(u)$ alone, which is the frequency rule of \camp-v1 and of hot/cold partitioning. We solve Eq.~(\ref{eq:knap}) exactly by dynamic programming and use it as a baseline and as one SCP candidate; it is a proxy, and Section~\ref{sec:res-pins} shows when it is a poor one.

\paragraph{Pipeline planner}
$T_{\mathrm{plan}}(\mathcal P)$ is evaluated with a deterministic recurrence over one iteration repeated three times. Let $\hat c_j$ be the estimated compute time of access $j$, $L$ the time at which the link becomes free and $R=C_w-\sum_{\mathcal P}s_u$ the streaming buffer. A streamed access $j$ is fetched as early as the link is free and the buffer has room, i.e.\ once earlier streamed units have finished computing and released enough bytes; it then computes when both the previous layer and its own fetch have finished:
\begin{align}
f_j &= \max(L,\ \rho_j) + t_{\mathrm{copy}}(s_j), & L &\leftarrow f_j,\\
b_j &= \max(e_{j-1},\ f_j), & e_j &= b_j + \hat c_j ,
\end{align}
where $\rho_j$ is the earliest time at which the buffer holds at most $R-s_j$ bytes of streamed units, and pinned accesses skip the fetch. The planner costs $O(|Q|)$ per evaluation.

\paragraph{Search and verification}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:scp} shortlists structured candidates (no pinning, prefix, frequency-ranked, knapsack-DP, evenly interleaved ``stride'' sets at five pinned fractions, and the lazy marginal-gain greedy set), scores them with $T_{\mathrm{plan}}$, refines the best by add/remove/local-swap search and a short simulated-annealing polish (about 1\,200 planner evaluations), and finally replays the shortlisted sets in the event engine for three iterations \emph{using the cost model's estimated layer times}, never the device's true times, and keeps the fastest replay. The replay removes planner approximations, such as ignoring buffer reuse across iterations, from the final decision. Planning takes 0.2--0.7~s for 160-unit models on one core and is performed once per operating point (or after calibration), not per token.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Stall-cost-aware pinning (SCP)}
\label{alg:scp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} unit sequence with sizes, cost model $\hat t$, link model $t_{\mathrm{copy}}$, capacity $C_w$
\State $\mathcal S \gets \{\emptyset\}\cup\{\textsc{Stride}(g): g\in\{.3,.45,.6,.75,.9\}\}\cup\{\textsc{Prefix},\textsc{Freq},\textsc{KnapsackDP},\textsc{Greedy}\}$ \Comment{feasible sets only}
\State $\mathcal P^\ast \gets \arg\min_{\mathcal P\in\mathcal S}\, T_{\mathrm{plan}}(\mathcal P)$
\State $\mathcal P^\ast \gets \textsc{LocalSearch}(\mathcal P^\ast)$ \Comment{add / remove / swap within $\pm3$ positions}
\State $\mathcal P^\ast \gets \textsc{Anneal}(\mathcal P^\ast,\ 1200\text{ evaluations})$
\State $\mathcal S \gets \mathcal S\cup\{\mathcal P^\ast\}$
\For{$\mathcal P\in\mathcal S$}
  \State $\tau(\mathcal P) \gets$ replay 3 iterations in the event engine with estimated layer times
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} $\arg\min_{\mathcal P\in\mathcal S}\tau(\mathcal P)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Why placement matters}
Consider $N$ equal units with compute $c$ and fetch time $f>c$, a streaming buffer holding $m\ge 2$ units, and $P$ pinned units. If the pinned units form a contiguous prefix, the link can only run $m-1$ units ahead while they compute, and idles for the rest:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{prefix}}\approx (N-P)\,f + \max\{0,\ P\,c-(m-1)f\},
\qquad
T_{\mathrm{interleaved}}\approx \max\{(N-P)\,f,\ N\,c\}.
\label{eq:placement}
\end{equation}
The penalty vanishes when $c\ll f$ (decode), and is largest when compute and transfer are comparable. Frequency-ranked and prefix pinning, which break ties by sequence position, generate exactly the contiguous case; Section~\ref{sec:res-pins} measures the link utilisation they lose.

\paragraph{Transient layers and eviction}
Units that are not pinned are evicted by next-use order (furthest next use first), which is optimal for the known order \cite{belady1966study}; the admission rule of Algorithm~\ref{alg:prefetch} prevents a prefetch from displacing a nearer use. On a cyclic scan with equal frequencies, LRU, FIFO and LFU all evict the layer that will be needed soonest and retain nothing (Section~\ref{sec:res-reuse}).

\section{Evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Simulator}
\label{sec:sim}

The simulator (\texttt{camp\_sim}, released with this paper) is an event-driven model of one GPU, one copy engine and an HBM weight cache. It contains no policy: prefetch, victim selection and pinning are plug-ins. The compute stream executes accesses strictly in order; each access first issues its demand fetch, then lets the policy issue lookahead, then waits for the weights. Copy completions and layer completions are events, and policies are re-invoked at both. Noise is generated per seed; the bandwidth drift is indexed by simulated time, so different policies run with the same seed see the same environment (common random numbers), and policy comparisons use paired seeds. All experiments use ten seeds unless stated; we report means and 95\% confidence half-widths ($t$-distribution). Layer weights are never materialised: the simulator tracks bytes, not tensors.

Three things are checked independently of the experiments. (i) \texttt{tests/test\_engine.py} compares the deterministic engine with closed-form pipeline times (demand paging $N(f+c)$; prefetch with a ring buffer $N\max(f,c)$; pinned layers $(N-P)f$; compute-bound $Nc$), which agree to within 2\%. (ii) The analytic planner agrees with the engine to a mean absolute error of 0.18\% (maximum 3.3\%) over 80 random model/workload/cache/link configurations (Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}). (iii) The pipeline logic is compared with a real GPU (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

\subsection{Hardware validation of the pipeline logic}
\label{sec:validation}

The simulator's novel parts are the copy path and the overlap of transfers with compute. We validate them on a real GPU with CUDA streams and events (\texttt{validation/hw\_validate.py}, run on a Tesla T4, PCIe Gen3 x16, CUDA 13). Twenty-four weight tensors of 64~MiB live in page-locked host memory, standing in for the CXL pool; layer $u$ computes a GEMM of $T$ tokens against its tensor. The loop supports a configurable prefetch depth (ring of device slots, events for slot reuse and copy completion) and a number of tensors pinned in HBM. We measure, \emph{in the same loop}, (a) a compute-only iteration (all tensors resident) for each $T$, (b) a copy-only iteration, and (c) 30 composite configurations (five $T$ values $\times$ six combinations of depth and pinned count), three repetitions each. The simulator is then composed from the separately measured compute time per unit and the fitted copy cost (12.4~GB/s, 10.6~$\mu$s) and asked to predict the composites. No parameter is fitted to the composites.

\input{tab_hw.tex}

Figure~\ref{fig:hw} and Table~\ref{tab:hw} give the result. Across the 30 configurations the mean absolute error is 7.6\% (median 5.6\%). In the link-bound regime, where the pipeline logic dominates, the error is 4.0\% on average and the 9 configurations with prefetch depth $\ge1$, $T\le1024$ and no pinned tensors are within 1.3\%. The largest errors (up to 25\%) occur where compute time is a large share and the simulator's constant compute time per unit is wrong: on this power-capped 70~W GPU, the same GEMM took about half as long when interleaved with copies at low duty cycle (demand paging) as in the back-to-back compute-only loop, and about 6\% longer under sustained load, a clock and power effect that the simulator does not model. The result supports the copy path, queueing and overlap logic; it does not validate the absolute compute times of an H100 or any CXL device, which we take from the roofline model and published measurements.

\subsection{Workloads, baselines and metrics}
\label{sec:baselines}

Workloads use the models of Table~\ref{tab:models}. For decode we use $B=16$ sequences with 2k-token contexts (70B: KV capacity reserved for 4k), for prefill $B\in\{1,4\}$ prompts of 2k or 4k tokens, and for mixed iterations 64 decode tokens plus a 512-token prefill chunk; the 13B/9B models are given 40\% of their weight bytes as cache (as on a GPU shared with other tenants or a large KV cache), the 70B model the HBM left after its KV cache. Non-uniform reuse is evaluated with Gemma-2-9B (tied embedding and head), speculative decoding (13B target, 1B draft, $k=4$ draft steps per verification) and a 24+24-layer encoder--decoder processing 64 output tokens per request.

Baselines (all given the same cache capacity and link): \textbf{Demand+LRU} (no prefetch); \textbf{Static-$k$} (fixed lookahead, best $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$ chosen per configuration, LRU); \textbf{Reactive} (lookahead grows on observed stalls, decays when stall-free; a pressure-driven, TMO-like controller \cite{tmo}); \textbf{Aggressive-10} (fixed deep lookahead, Limoncello-like \cite{limoncello}); \textbf{FlexGen-style} (resident layer prefix, double-buffered streaming \cite{sheng2023flexgen}); \textbf{Hot/Cold} (resident set chosen by access frequency, ties by position, streaming with lookahead 2; PowerInfer-style partitioning \cite{song2024powerinfer} adapted to streaming); \textbf{\camp-v1} (the earlier formulation: lookahead gated by the compute estimate, frequency pinning); and \textbf{\camp-v2} (this paper). These are reimplementations of the \emph{mechanisms} in the common simulator, not ports of the original systems. \camp-v2 uses the cost model calibrated after one probe iteration and SCP with dry-run verification; variants with the generic (uncalibrated) and the exact device model are reported where relevant.

The metric is the steady-state iteration latency (the first iteration is a warm-up and excluded), compared with the lower bound of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lb}).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Regime map: when does streaming from CXL pay off?}
\label{sec:res-regime}

Figure~\ref{fig:regime} gives the latency of \camp{}-managed streaming relative to a GPU with all weights resident, for Llama-2-13B with 40\% of the weights cached, as a function of tokens per iteration and of $\bwlink$. At 18~GB/s the slowdown is 79$\times$ for one token, 77$\times$ at 64 tokens, 66$\times$ at 256, 19$\times$ at 1\,024, 4.6$\times$ at 4\,096, 1.14$\times$ at 16\,384 and 1.01$\times$ at 65\,536. At 45~GB/s the same rows read 32$\times$, 32$\times$, 27.5$\times$, 7.8$\times$, 1.9$\times$, 1.01$\times$ and 1.00$\times$. The transition follows Eq.~(\ref{eq:threshold}), $T^\star\approx 20\mathrm{k}$ tokens at 18~GB/s and 8\,000 at 45~GB/s. Two consequences follow. Prefill of long prompts and very large batches can run from CXL-resident weights at close to resident speed, so capacity can be traded for little latency. Interactive decode cannot: with at most 256 tokens per iteration the slowdown is between 19$\times$ (256 tokens, 64~GB/s) and 181$\times$ (one token, 8~GB/s), and the only lever is to move fewer bytes. Neither prefetching nor cleverer replacement changes this; a larger cache, a faster link or compression do \cite{xie2025trace}.

\subsection{Main comparison}
\label{sec:res-main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:main} compare the policies in 16 configurations (three models, decode, mixed and prefill iterations, two link bandwidths). Three observations.

\emph{\camp-v2 is close to the lower bound everywhere.} Its latency is 0.996--1.058$\times$ the bound of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lb}) in all 16 configurations (values just below one are noise around a deterministic bound), with the largest gaps in the two balanced 18~GB/s prefill configurations (1.054 and 1.058).

\emph{Against published-style baselines the gain depends on the regime.} In link-bound decode and short prefill, where the number of resident bytes decides everything, \camp-v2 is 1.04--1.11$\times$ faster than the FlexGen-style and Hot/Cold baselines and 1.6--1.8$\times$ faster than demand paging or any LRU-based prefetcher, which retain nothing on a cyclic scan. In the balanced prefill configurations (13B $B{=}4,S{=}4$k at 18~GB/s; 70B prefill at 18 and 45~GB/s) it is 1.26--1.47$\times$ faster than FlexGen-style and Hot/Cold, 1.23--1.40$\times$ faster than \camp-v1, and 2.4--2.9$\times$ faster than demand paging. In compute-bound configurations (13B and 70B, 4k-token prefill at 45~GB/s) the best static baselines already reach the bound and \camp-v2 matches them (within 1\%); in the 13B case the Hot/Cold baseline is 29\% slower because its pinned set and fixed lookahead leave too small a streaming buffer (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}).

\emph{The tied-embedding model benefits from a different pin set.} For Gemma-2-9B, \camp-v2 is 1.29$\times$ faster than FlexGen-style and Hot/Cold. The reason is mechanical: the baselines reserve two slots of the largest unit (the 1.8~GB embedding/head table) for streaming, which caps what they can pin at 3.7~GB of 7.4~GB; SCP pins the table itself and keeps 7.0~GB resident.

\subsection{Pinning: what does the selection rule buy?}
\label{sec:res-pins}

Figure~\ref{fig:pins} compares pin-selection rules with the prefetcher fixed (CAMP's controller, next-use eviction): no pinning, prefix, frequency-ranked (the pinning rule of \camp-v1), random, interleaved stride, the exact knapsack of Eq.~(\ref{eq:knap}), and SCP.

\emph{Link-bound iterations are insensitive to the rule.} In decode and short prefill every rule that pins about 9--10~GB lands within 3\% of the others (13B decode at 18~GB/s: frequency 898~ms, random 899~ms, stride 909~ms, knapsack 891~ms, SCP 873~ms); what matters is the number of resident bytes. SCP's edge of about 3\% there comes from packing: it keeps 9.9~GB resident where the conservative reserve of the other rules allows 9.3~GB.

\emph{Balanced iterations are very sensitive to placement.} In the three 18~GB/s prefill configurations with compute comparable to transfer, frequency-ranked pinning is 1.23--1.40$\times$ slower than SCP, and even random pinning is 13--25\% faster than frequency ranking. For 13B prefill ($B{=}4,S{=}4$k) the link utilisation is 79--80\% for prefix and frequency pins (which pin a contiguous run of early layers, so the link runs ahead by only a few units while they compute) against 99.5--100\% for random, stride and SCP sets; the iteration times are 1\,167 versus 927--935~ms in the deterministic engine. This is the mechanism of Eq.~(\ref{eq:placement}). The exact knapsack (DP) packs more bytes and beats frequency ranking, but remains 6--31\% slower than SCP because its benefit ignores overlap. In the compute-bound 4k-token prefill configurations at 45~GB/s all rules are within 4\% of each other and of no pinning; the 70B 2k-token prefill at 45~GB/s is balanced again, and frequency ranking is 25\% slower than SCP there.

\emph{Optimality.} On an 18-unit model, where all $2^{18}$ pin sets can be enumerated, SCP is on average 0.7\% (maximum 4.2\%) above the exhaustive optimum of the planner objective, versus 21.1\% (maximum 32.4\%) for frequency ranking (Table~\ref{tab:gap}); replayed in the event engine the mean gap is 2.7\% and the maximum 12.8\%, which marks the planner's approximation error that the dry-run verification reduces but does not remove. SCP is a heuristic with no optimality guarantee; the simple stride rule, which needs no planner, is within 1\% of SCP in six of the twelve cases and within 4.5\% in eleven of them (8.2\% for 70B 2k-token prefill at 18~GB/s).

\subsection{Non-uniform reuse and eviction}
\label{sec:res-reuse}

\paragraph{Eviction on a uniform loop}
Table~\ref{tab:evict} shows eviction policies on 13B decode with no pinning. LRU, FIFO and LFU produce identical results and zero hits: on a cyclic scan with equal access counts, LFU has no frequency signal to exploit and degenerates to LRU. Random eviction achieves 7\% hits, next-use eviction 32.5\%, and the latter matters because it is the only policy that retains some layers at all. A tie between LFU and graph-aware eviction can nevertheless appear when a model has tied embeddings, the only layers with a frequency difference (Gemma-2-9B below); it reflects that single signal, not an equivalence of the two mechanisms.

\input{tab_evict.tex}

\paragraph{Workloads with genuinely different reuse}
Table~\ref{tab:hetero} and Figure~\ref{fig:hetero} give three cases where the number of accesses per iteration differs between layers. For Gemma-2-9B with a tied embedding, demand paging with LFU is 10\% faster than LRU (the embedding is accessed twice per step), prefix, frequency and knapsack pinning give 0.81~s, and SCP 0.63~s thanks to pinning the large tied table. In speculative decoding the draft model's layers are used $k=4$ times per iteration; frequency pins already capture this (1.03~s versus 1.01~s for SCP), and LFU is 6\% faster than LRU. For the encoder--decoder, encoder layers are used once per request and decoder layers 64 times: prefix pinning is useless (27.0~s), LRU and LFU are identical (27.8~s, since no layer is hit often enough for LFU to separate them), while frequency pins (10.2~s) and knapsack/SCP pins (9.1~s) are 2.7--3.0$\times$ faster. Reuse awareness is therefore decisive when per-layer frequencies differ by large factors; it is not when they are uniform, and the cases in which \camp's pin rule differs from frequency ranking by more than a few percent are those with a very large unit (Gemma) or comparable compute and transfer (Section~\ref{sec:res-pins}).

\subsection{How much does the compute estimate matter?}
\label{sec:res-cost}

Table~\ref{tab:costmodel} and Fig.~\ref{fig:cost} address how $\hat t_u$ is obtained and what happens when it is wrong. The generic roofline estimate has a mean absolute error of 23\% for prefill and 9\% for decode against the simulated device; one probe iteration reduces this to 0.5--2.7\%. Prefetching is insensitive to the estimate: with the original compute-gated controller and with the work-conserving controller, and with estimate biases from 0.25$\times$ to 4$\times$, the latencies are identical (919~ms, 3\,788~ms and 873~ms for the three workloads). The pinning stage is sensitive in the balanced regime when the estimate is uncalibrated: for 13B prefill, SCP planned from an estimate with bias 0.5 or 0.25 gives 966 or 988~ms instead of 920~ms (+5\% and +7\%), and per-layer log-normal noise with $\sigma=0.5$ adds up to 9\% at bias~1; for 70B prefill a bias of 2 or 4 gives 4\,712 or 4\,671~ms instead of 3\,788~ms, a 23--24\% loss. After online calibration the spread over the whole bias range shrinks to 907--929~ms (13B) and 3\,770--3\,788~ms (70B), below 3\% and still below 3\% with $\sigma=0.5$ noise. Decode is insensitive throughout (870--875~ms) because it is link-bound. In summary, \camp{} needs the compute estimate only for pinning and only in the balanced regime, and an exponential moving average over one iteration of observed layer times is enough.

\subsection{Batching, model size and link sensitivity}
\label{sec:res-scale}

\paragraph{Batching}
Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} shows throughput. For decode with 512-token contexts (13B, 40\% cached, 18~GB/s) \camp{} reaches 1.1, 18.4, 73.5, 292 and 583 tokens/s at $B=1,16,64,256,512$: throughput grows linearly with the batch because the streamed bytes per iteration are constant and compute is negligible, and the slowdown relative to resident weights shrinks from 79$\times$ to 9.0$\times$ at $B=512$. Resident weights saturate at about 5\,200 tokens/s (compute- and KV-bound) beyond $B\approx256$. In the GEMM-batch view the throughput of \camp{} saturates at the compute-bound limit (17\,000 tokens/s) only at about 16\,000 tokens per iteration. Batching amortises weight traffic, as in resident-weight engines, but the amortisation continues for two orders of magnitude longer in streaming mode because it starts from a link-bound state.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the results mean for CXL-backed inference}
With an effective bandwidth of 10--20~GB/s per expander, streaming weights from CXL is practical for compute-dense iterations (long-prompt prefill, very large batches) and impractical for interactive decode, where the slowdown against resident weights is tens of times and prefetching cannot help because the iteration is link-bound. For decode, the achievable gains come from the number of resident bytes and the packing of the cache (Section~\ref{sec:res-pins}), and from raising $\bwlink$ (more expanders, page-locked DMA, compression). \camp{} reaches the lower bound in link-bound configurations, so there is little room for further policy work there; the remaining room is in balanced iterations (up to 6\% above the bound at 18~GB/s) and in the planner's approximation error.

\paragraph{Limitations}
(i) \emph{No CXL hardware.} The CXL link is simulated; its bandwidth and fixed costs are taken from published measurements \cite{yoon2025tract,jang2026itme} and swept over a wide range, and the pipeline logic is validated on a PCIe-attached T4 with pinned host DRAM in place of a CXL pool. We did not measure a real expander, a PCIe Gen5 GPU or an H100; the compute times of the H100-class device come from a roofline model with efficiencies that we did not measure. (ii) \emph{Power and clock effects.} On the T4 the compute time of a layer depends on duty cycle by up to a factor of two, an effect absent from the simulator; it causes the largest validation errors (up to 25\%) where compute and copy are comparable, which is exactly where SCP's gains are, so the quantitative gains in the balanced regime should be read with this uncertainty. (iii) \emph{Scope of the models.} We evaluate dense transformers with a static order. Mixture-of-Experts routing makes the access order data-dependent; hot/cold partitioning by observed frequency \cite{song2024powerinfer} is the natural baseline and an online variant of SCP is future work. (iv) \emph{Single GPU, single tenant.} Multi-GPU sharing of one CXL pool, link contention between tenants and CXL switches are not modelled; . (v) \emph{The KV cache stays in HBM}; offloading it to CXL \cite{yoon2025tract,lee2024infinigen} competes for the same link. (vi) \emph{SCP is a heuristic.} It is within 4.2\% of the exhaustive optimum of its own objective on an 18-unit model, but there is no guarantee for larger models, and in the event engine the pin set it selects is up to 12.8\% slower than the exhaustive optimum of the planner objective, an approximation error that the dry-run replay mitigates but does not remove. (vii) \emph{Baselines are reimplementations} of mechanisms, not ports of FlexGen, PowerInfer, TMO or Limoncello, and the Hot/Cold baseline's streaming buffer rule (two slots of the largest unit) is a choice that disadvantages it when one unit is very large. (viii) \emph{Validation depth.} We do not use a cycle-accurate simulator; GPU kernels are black boxes at the level of detail we need, and CXL-DMSim-style device models \cite{wang2025cxldmsim} are complementary. The cost of re-planning pins when the batch descriptor changes materially (moving up to the pinned bytes once, about 0.5~s for 9~GB at 18~GB/s) is not charged in the experiments.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied LLM inference with weights streamed from CXL expanders through a host-mediated DMA path, showed with a closed-form threshold and a regime map when this is nearly free (above about 16k tokens per iteration) and when it is tens of times slower (decode), and presented \camp, a policy built from an online-calibrated cost model, a work-conserving prefetcher with next-use admission and stall-cost-aware pinning. The pipeline machinery of the simulator was validated on a real GPU (7.6\% mean error, 4.0\% when link-bound). In 16 configurations \camp{} stays within 6\% of an implementation-independent lower bound and is up to 1.47$\times$ faster than the best static baseline. Its advantage over a frequency-based pinning rule is small in link-bound decode and large when compute and transfer are comparable, because contiguous pinned sets idle the link; LFU gives no protection on cyclic scans, and reuse-aware pinning is decisive when per-layer reuse genuinely differs, as in encoder--decoder inference. The main open items are measurement on real CXL hardware and PCIe Gen5 GPUs, online variants for Mixture-of-Experts, and multi-tenant sharing of the pool.
