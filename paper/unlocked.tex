To see whether the conclusions survive at default clocks, we repeated the Llama-3.2-3B prefill of 256, 512 and 1\,024 tokens without a clock lock ($r=0,7,14,21$, $k=2,3$, \nUnlockedN{} streaming configurations). The mean SM clock was 1\,368, 1\,114 and 680~MHz for the three lengths, so the GPU computed two to three times faster than in the locked runs and moved closer to its power limit as the work grew. The qualitative result holds: the balanced placement was never more than 1\% slower than the contiguous one, and the contiguous placement was up to \nUnlockedPenaltyMax$\times$ slower (prefill of 256 tokens, $r=21$, $k=2$). The quantitative model holds much less well. Its mean error is \nUnlockedMape\% (maximum \nUnlockedMax\%), and it overestimates the cost of contiguous placement: for the configuration above it predicts 1.46 instead of the measured \nUnlockedPenaltyMax. Correcting the compute times by the measured mean clock of each run makes the error larger, not smaller (mean \nUnlockedMapeClk\%), because the clock varies within an iteration: when the copy engine waits during a resident block, the GPU draws less power and its clock rises, which shortens exactly the part of the iteration that the model charges to contiguous placement. At default clocks on a power-limited GPU the model therefore ranks the placements correctly but overstates their difference.
